How your score is worked out

  1. Every sound gets a score from 0 to 100. A model trained on thousands of sounds scored by pronunciation experts looks at how confidently each expected sound was heard, what else it could have been, and how long it lasted.
  2. Your overall score is the average of those sound scores. A sentence has 20–30 sounds, so one or two weak sounds lower it only a little. That is why you can see 92 with two words marked.
  3. Colours come from a separate check. A sound is marked needs work only when the recogniser heard a different sound, fluent Indian-English speakers don't say it that way, and the score is also low. If the score is still high it is almost. Standard Indian-English pronunciations are gold and never count against you.

How much to trust it: on held-out learner speech the sound scores agree with human experts at a correlation of 0.68 (1.0 would be perfect). Use it as a practice guide, not an exam grade. The count of clear sounds is the easiest number to track.

How PhonoLens works

  1. Listen. A wav2vec2 model fine-tuned on non-native English writes down the sounds you actually made, every 20 ms.
  2. Compare. Your sounds are lined up with the dictionary pronunciation, so each difference reads as "expected /v/, heard /w/".
  3. Score. Goodness-of-pronunciation features feed a model trained on expert scores, giving every sound a 0–100 score.
  4. Judge fairly. Sounds that fluent Indian-English speakers produce, such as a dropped final /r/, are never counted as errors.
  5. Measure vowels. Classical LPC analysis measures F1 and F2 and places your vowels next to fluent Indian-English targets.

Recordings are analysed on this server and not stored. The reference voice is generated by an online text-to-speech service from the sentence text only. Progress history stays in this browser.
